A control bot has **no active weapon**. It wins by getting under opponents,
controlling their movement, and exposing them to arena hazards — and by
surviving to a judges' decision on control and aggression.

Even weapon robots need good wedge geometry on the front, so this page applies
broadly.

## Why wedges work

A wedge that slides under the opponent:

- transfers the opponent's weight onto **your** [driven wheels](/fundamentals/traction/),
  raising your pushing force and lowering theirs,
- lifts their wheels off the floor so they can't drive,
- presents your armored underside/wedge to their weapon instead of your
  vulnerable sides,
- feeds them into your weapon, if you have one.

## Geometry

- **Front-edge height:** as low as the arena floor allows. Sub-millimeter gaps
  are normal for competitive wedges. Arena floor seams and your own flex set
  the practical limit.
- **Wedge angle:** shallow (10–30°) for getting under; steeper resists being
  wedged in return but is easier to get under. Multi-angle wedges compromise.
- **Coverage:** full robot width. Gaps at the corners are where opponents get a
  purchase on you.
- **Rigidity:** a wedge that flexes up under load loses its gap. Back it with
  ribs or a hardened plate.
- **Surface:** hardened, smooth, low-friction on top (opponent slides up) and
  well-supported underneath.

## Ground clearance and skids

The wedge tip and hardened skids define ride height. Set it so:

- the front edge bites under opponents,
- the robot still drives when [inverted](/drivetrain/layouts/#invertible-driving),
- you don't high-center on arena floor seams or the kill saws' slots.

## Materials

Spring steel, hardened tool steel, or hardened stainless for the working edge;
UHMW or HDPE for low-friction surfaces; titanium where mass matters more than
edge retention.[^riobotz]
