A control bot has **no active weapon**. It wins by getting under opponents,
controlling their movement, exposing them to arena hazards, and surviving to a
judges' decision on damage, aggression, and control.

**Ground game** — whatever gets you under the opponent — decides most head-on
engagements, so it matters on weapon robots too. Being underneath breaks the
opponent's traction, lifts their wheels, points your armor at their weapon, and
disables most of their weapon types while enabling yours.[^b4d]

## Why getting under works

Sliding under the opponent:

- transfers their weight onto **your** [driven wheels](/fundamentals/traction/),
  raising your pushing force and lowering theirs,
- lifts their wheels off the floor so they can't drive,
- presents your armored underside to their weapon instead of your vulnerable
  sides,
- feeds them into your weapon, if you have one.

## Forks — the modern standard

Long narrow prongs that ride on the floor ahead of the robot. Their small
contact patch is easy to keep flush, so they get under almost anything —
including wedges, which is why forks displaced wedges as the primary ground-game
tool.[^b4d]

- **Sharp forks** — short and pointy, mostly on vertical spinners. Guide the
  opponent into the weapon and stop wedges getting under; out-reached by longer
  forks, so paired with weapons that already counter long forks (drums,
  eggbeaters) or with fast flanking drive.
- **Reach forks** — long, optimised purely for getting under and controlling.
  On head-on hits they slide into the gaps between the opponent's shorter forks
  and lift first. Heavy and vulnerable from the side; common on lifters and
  overhead bots.
- **Corralling forks** — long forks on the front corners, often soft plastic.
  Stop opponents escaping a pin or getting to your sides, and widen your
  footprint for stability.

Bring spare forks to every event — they wear and bend.[^b4d]

## Wedgelets

A middle ground: extra-wide forks or angled plates covering more of the front.
More durable than forks and better against off-plane hits, but they struggle to
get under real forks. Used like sharp forks.

## Wedges

A full sloped front face. Simple and durable, but the large floor contact makes
it hard to keep the whole edge flush, so narrower forks slide under it. **Don't
rely on a plain wedge to get under a modern opponent.**[^b4d]

Wedges do have one strong modern use: **anti-horizontal armor.** A hard, flat,
upward-angled face — usually bent [titanium](/weapons/spinner-types/) — deflects
a horizontal spinner's blade upward instead of taking the bite, and most
horizontals carry no ground game to punish the wedge in return.[^b4d]

## Wedge / fork geometry

- **Front-edge height:** as low as the arena floor allows. Sub-millimetre gaps
  are normal on competitive forks. Arena floor seams and your own flex set the
  practical limit.
- **Angle:** shallow (10–30°) to get under; steeper resists being wedged in
  return.
- **Coverage:** watch the corners — gaps there are where opponents get a
  purchase.
- **Rigidity:** a fork or wedge that flexes up under load loses its gap. Back it
  with ribs, a stiffener plate, or a hardened edge.
- **Surface:** hard and smooth on top so the opponent slides up; well-supported
  underneath.

## Downforce magnets

On steel-floor arenas, neodymium magnets in the baseplate add normal force
without adding weight the way ballast would — more traction for pushing and more
resistance to being flipped. Common on control bots and wedges; set wheel
hardness and ground clearance to suit the extra pull.[^b4d]

## Ground clearance and skids

The front edge and hardened skids define ride height. Set it so:

- the front edge bites under opponents,
- the robot still drives when [inverted](/drivetrain/layouts/#invertible-driving),
- you don't high-centre on floor seams or the kill-saw slots.

## Materials

AR steel or spring/tool steel for the working edge of forks; bent 6Al-4V
(Grade 5) titanium for anti-horizontal wedges; UHMW, HDPE, or TPU for
low-friction and soft corralling elements.[^riobotz][^b4d]
